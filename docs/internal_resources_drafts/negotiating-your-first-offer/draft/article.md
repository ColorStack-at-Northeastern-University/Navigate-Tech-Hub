# How to negotiate your first internship offer without blowing the relationship

The call comes in, or the email lands, and your brain short-circuits between relief and panic. You want the offer. You also do not want to leave money or clarity on the table, especially if you are paying Boston rent on a student budget. For a lot of Black and Latinx students, there is an extra voice in the background: *If I push, will they pull the offer?* Structured asks are normal in hiring. Skip the fantasy of “winning” a recruiter argument. Align the package with market reality and what you actually need from the summer, then either accept on terms you understand or walk away with your dignity intact.

## What “negotiation” means for interns

You are negotiating a bundle, not a single number: hourly pay or stipend, start date, relocation or housing support, signing or one-time bonuses, equipment or learning stipends, and plain-language clarity on what you will work on and who supports you. [Levels.fyi’s negotiation guide](https://www.levels.fyi/blog/ultimate-negotiation-guide.html) and [CodePath’s internship salary section](https://books.codepath.org/student-handbook/offer-and-salary-negotiation/negotiating-on-internship-salary) both say the same thing in different voices: total rewards, a few levers, organized notes, polite tone.

Before you argue for anything, read the written offer. Verbal enthusiasm is cheap; numbers and dates in a PDF or DocuSign envelope are what you are signing. If something the recruiter said does not appear in writing, assume it is not real until it is.

## The first two days: thank, clarify, buy time

You do not owe anyone a yes on the phone. Thank them, express genuine interest, and ask for the written package and a clear decision timeline. Take notes on anything numeric: pay frequency, housing, bonuses, level or band if they mention it. After you read the document, you can ask for a short follow-up call if anything is fuzzy.

If you are on Northeastern co-op, the clock is tighter than generic “sleep on it” posts. Northeastern’s public co-op overview under “Accepting an offer” says to **acknowledge offers within 48 hours**, work with your coordinator because **acceptance commits you**, and international students should **check visa requirements** ([The co-op process, from start to finish](https://www.northeastern.edu/the-co-op-process-from-start-to-finish/)). Reply fast, confirm receipt, put questions in writing, and keep “I got it” separate from “I accept” until you mean it.

If the employer sets an “exploding” deadline, ask why and request a short extension for a real decision. Calm, specific asks often get a yes ([Levels.fyi](https://www.levels.fyi/blog/ultimate-negotiation-guide.html)). If the date is impossible, say so early instead of ghosting.

## Build one primary ask, grounded outside your rent

Research before you counter. Pull anchors for the same role flavor, company stage, and location using tools such as company pages on Levels.fyi and honest signals from peers (without gossiping offer letters you should not have seen). Intern pay spreads wide by hub and employer; guessing undersells you and overshooting reads sloppy.

Keep personal budget math private. Your rent is your floor for your own decision. Out loud, lead with market bands and the value you bring: skills, pace to impact, relevant projects. Employers hear “my bills” as pressure; they hear “here is the band for this work in this city” as preparation ([Levels.fyi](https://www.levels.fyi/blog/ultimate-negotiation-guide.html); [Latinx in Tech Careers on negotiation tone](https://latinxintechcareers.com/resources/how-to-negotiate-salary-without-losing-the-offer)). CodePath’s student flow still mentions outlining expenses internally. Keep that in your spreadsheet. Open the conversation with market bands and what you bring, and keep rent out of the recruiter thread.

Pick **one** primary lever first (base, signing bonus, relocation, start date). Propose a **range**, not a single awkward integer. Collaborative language works: excitement first, then “I’d love to explore flexibility on X based on what I’m seeing in the market for similar roles.” Pause after you speak. Ultimatums and fabricating competing offers are how you lose trust; some employers ask for proof, and lying is an automatic L ([Levels.fyi](https://www.levels.fyi/blog/ultimate-negotiation-guide.html)).

Many employers treat negotiation as routine; plenty of candidates still never ask. A respectful counter does not need a wall of statistics behind it.

## Co-op, CPT, and when “small” changes are not small

For F-1 students at Northeastern, co-op is not a casual side gig. CPT is **employer-, location-, and date-specific**; authorization must exist **before** start; OGS targets **fifteen business days** after a complete CPT e-form reaches them ([OGS CPT page](https://international.northeastern.edu/ogs/employment/curricular-practical-training-cpt-f1/)). Changing employer, site, hours, or start date after paperwork is moving triggers coordinator and OGS updates, not a quick email to the recruiter. Flag shifts **early**.

Khoury adds: summer extensions need coordinator approval **two weeks** ahead, I-20 dates track CPT updates, and supplemental work on co-op needs OGS clearance first ([Khoury undergraduate co-op policies](https://www.khoury.northeastern.edu/undergraduate-co-op-policies/)). If it touches dates or authorization, treat it as **process**, not vibes.

## Scope, mentors, and onboarding

Pay is one row on the spreadsheet. A bad summer is unclear goals, a missing mentor, and onboarding that never shows up. [People of Color in Tech’s intern piece](https://peopleofcolorintech.com/front/welcome-to-intern-life-negotiate-your-experience/) makes the case for negotiating the experience itself: goals, mentor access, onboarding, how success is measured. Ask about those in the same thread as comp so you read as someone who plans to ship work, not someone who only scanned the dollar line.

## If the answer is no

Tone beats whether one lever moves. CodePath-style guidance: respectful asks rarely rescind offers; if pay is fixed, you can usually accept the original package. Thank them, confirm next steps in writing, and check the letter against what you heard on the phone ([Levels.fyi](https://www.levels.fyi/blog/ultimate-negotiation-guide.html)). If base is locked, try signing bonus, housing, equipment budget, or a performance revisit. Warmth keeps the bridge; bitterness burns it.

## Your homework in the next twenty-four hours

Draft a five-line email you have **not** sent yet: one line of gratitude, two clarification questions on written terms, one data-grounded flexibility ask **or** a request to reconnect after you finish reading the packet, and a clean sign-off. Pull at least one external comp anchor before you send anything. That single draft is the difference between panic-accepting on the phone and walking into your summer like someone who reads contracts.

**What you’ll leave with**

- A first-offer rhythm: defer instant acceptance, read the written terms, benchmark, pick one primary ask, confirm details in email.
- Language that sounds collaborative: excitement, range, market basis, silence after the ask.
- Non-cash levers if base is fixed: signing bonus, relocation, housing, equipment or learning budget, start date, scope and mentor clarity.
