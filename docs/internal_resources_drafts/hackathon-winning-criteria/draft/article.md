# What Winning Hackathon Teams Do Differently

You shipped something you are proud of, then walked out wondering why the team with the slicker deck placed higher. Raw talent rarely sorts that outcome on its own. **Criteria literacy** and **demo time economics** explain most of the spread. Organizers publish how you will be scored. Winning teams treat that text like a spec and build backward from it.

## How judging actually runs (and why it feels tight)

In-person MLH-style expos often run science-fair rotations. The organizer-facing [MLH judging plan](https://guide.mlh.io/general-information/judging-and-submissions/judging-plan) tells volunteers they only have **three minutes per team** on a strict timeline. Judges are asked to stack-rank a **top three** from the projects they are assigned, with points three / two / one for first through third in that set. That keeps scores comparable when each judge only sees a handful of teams. Your ranking reflects the slice that judge saw in one pass on a hard clock. Nobody stacks you against every team in the venue in a single walk-through.

Devpost-backed online judging is a different shape: organizers define criteria, judges rate each line on a short scale (often one to five), and the help center notes that **built-in online judging assumes equally weighted criteria**. If weights differ, many events move scoring offline while still listing criteria on the site. Either way, the student move is the same: read the **Rules** and **Judging criteria** tabs before you lock scope.

MLH also pushes a **two-minute submission video** that **shows the product in use** rather than a slide narrated like a pitch deck. That is a hint about what “good” looks like when nobody is in the room to ask follow-ups: clickable proof beats narrated ambition.

## Decode the rubric before you name the repo

Generic advice about “innovation” is useless because the words on the page change. Two real 2026 examples:

- [QuHacks 2026](https://quhacks-2026.devpost.com/rules) (Howard Community College) lists **Creativity**, **Functionality**, and **Complexity**, each with a short judge-facing paragraph, plus a **Beginner** track with eligibility spelled out in the same doc.
- [Hack For Humanity 2026](https://hack-for-humanity-2026.devpost.com/rules) (Santa Clara University) uses **Technology**, **Polish**, **Innovativeness**, **Social Impact**, and **Project Completeness**, and states judges **weigh the criteria equally**.

Those labels are not the same as the “idea / implementation / impact” cluster you see in Devpost’s default online-judging documentation. Both patterns show up in the wild. Your job is to copy **your** event’s rows into a small table: criterion → what “good” looks like on that page → the evidence you will show (a demo step, a screenshot, a number) → who owns it. If the site says weights are uneven but scoring is offline, **budget build time to the heavy rows**, not to the part of the stack you wanted to learn anyway.

Sponsor tracks and category prizes usually mean **different judge panels** and different questions. The story that wins “best use of X API” is not automatically the story that wins “social impact.” Pick the track, then line up the demo so the first minute answers that track’s implied prompt.

## What to build when you only have a weekend

Student writeups and organizer guidance converge on a boring truth: **completeness beats complexity**. A smaller slice that runs end-to-end usually beats a wide surface that flakes under pressure. Plan one **primary user path** you can show in order: open the app, do the thing, show the result. Put architecture diagrams second, or in the README, because in a three-minute rotation the judge may never ask for them.

Treat the first thirty seconds like you will not get a second pass. Shortlists and staggered assignments mean some teams never get a do-over if the demo confuses people on walk-up. Run a timed dry run with someone who has not touched the code. Fix whatever they trip on first.

If there is a **Community Choice** or vote-based prize, read which awards use judges versus popularity. Optimizing for shares is a different game than optimizing for rubric rows. Do not mix the strategies by accident.

## Same discipline at affinity and beginner-forward events

Community-forward hackathons still publish criteria and tracks. Beginner prizes exist to reward honest scope and learning, not to excuse a broken core loop. The prep loop is identical: pull the posted rubric, align the demo, match the track language. MLH’s own judge briefing language leans **learning over profit**; “impact” in these rooms is often problem insight plus clever execution, not a revenue slide.

Before you open your editor tonight, paste your event’s criteria into a checklist and run a **three-minute timed demo** with a teammate who has not seen the project. Whatever they stumble on first is your P0 until it is not.
