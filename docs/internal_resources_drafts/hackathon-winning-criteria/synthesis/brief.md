DOMAIN ASSESSMENT

**Execution** (hackathons / shipping): practitioner organizer guides (MLH, Devpost) and student-facing retrospectives carry similar weight. The student-visible “winning” lever is almost always **published rubrics + demo economics under time pressure**, not generic motivation.

SOURCES SEARCHED

1. **MLH Hackathon Organizer Guide — Judging Plan** ([guide.mlh.io](https://guide.mlh.io/general-information/judging-and-submissions/judging-plan)) — science-fair-style judging; ~**3 minutes per team** (strict); recommend **2-minute demo video as demo not deck** for submissions; **stack ranking** (each judge’s top 3 per assigned set) to normalize scorer leniency; judge briefing emphasizes **learning over profit** and “top 3 of your set” workflow; logistical note that each project should be seen by multiple judges with staggered assignments.

2. **Devpost Help Center — Setting up judging** ([help.devpost.com/article/131-setting-up-judging](https://help.devpost.com/article/131-setting-up-judging)) — **online judging** uses organizer-defined criteria rated **1–5** per criterion; offline judging common for in-person demos/expos; **list all criteria publicly** when adding judges.

3. **Devpost Help Center — Judging & public voting** ([help.devpost.com/article/64-judging-public-voting](https://help.devpost.com/article/64-judging-public-voting)) — typical published criteria clusters: **quality of idea**, **implementation**, **potential impact**; **weights** matter but **Devpost’s built-in online judging assumes equally weighted criteria** — uneven weights → usually **offline** scoring with weights communicated on the site; **Community Choice** via public voting has fraud/gaming risks (prize sizing / not live results); organizer practice of **shortlisting** (e.g. cap ~30 for judge review) when volume is high.

4. **Hackathon planning / student guides (DEV)** — DoraHacks on **designing a judging plan**: **3–4 clear criteria**, evaluable questions, weights if priorities differ; **survival guide** theme: **completeness beats complexity** — runnable, clear MVP beats ambitious half-built scope; Arbitrum Open House recap: judges look for **demo–repo alignment**, working paths, and **problem–solution fit** over generic pitches.

5. **Audience layer — affinity / community hackathons (public Devpost pages)** — examples like **DivHacks**, **SolHacks**, and historical **Hack4Diversity (Code2040)** show **explicit prize tracks** (e.g. beginner) and published rubric language (impact, design, technical difficulty, pitch). **Signal for Navigate audience:** many students first “serious” events are **community-forward hackathons** with **the same prep loop** as generic MLH events—**read the posted rubric and track rules first**, then scope.

6. **Secondary practitioner summaries** (e.g. HackHQ, university hackathon pages) — repeat weighting patterns (**innovation, technical execution, impact, UX/demo, presentation**) with variable percentages; useful for examples, **not** a substitute for **your event’s posted criteria**.

KEY FINDINGS

[GEM] **Treat the published criteria as the spec.** Before you lock scope, copy the hackathon’s **criteria descriptions + prize/track rules** (Devpost “Rules” / “Judging criteria” or PDF) into a 4-row table: *criterion → what “good” looks like → evidence you will show (demo step / screenshot / metric) → owner.* If weights are only on the website (offline judging), **model your time budget to the weighted categories**, not to what feels technically coolest.

[GEM] **MLH-style expo judging is a ~3-minute comprehension test.** Plan a demo script that shows **one primary user path end-to-end** first; put “how it works” architecture second. Judges are time-boxed and often stack-rank **top 3** from their assigned walk—**clarity and a working core loop** beat breadth.

[GEM] **If the platform is Devpost online judging, assume three scored lenses** (idea / implementation / impact) unless the page says otherwise—and **make each lens explicit in the submission** (video + first paragraphs), because judges score **each criterion independently** on a short scale.

[GEM] **Submission video guidance (when required): demo, not a slide show.** MLH’s organizer guide explicitly pushes a **demo-forward** clip; student takeaway is to record **clickable proof**, not narrated ambition.

[GEM] **Completeness > complexity for many outcomes.** Multiple student-facing writeups converge on: a **smaller working slice** that is easy to try typically outperforms a **large, fragile surface area** under judge time constraints.

[GEM] **Community Choice is a different game.** If a prize is vote-based, the rubric is partly distribution and storytelling; **don’t confuse** vote optimization with technical judging—**read which prizes use judges vs popularity**.

Supporting point: MLH’s judge debrief text warns against treating hackathons as **startup pitch contests**; “impact” in judging is often **problem insight + clever execution**, not revenue claims.

Supporting point: Category/track prizes mean **different judge panels** (Devpost) — your **story should match the track** you selected, not a generic “we used AI.”

Supporting point: Organizer logistics (shortlists, staggered assignments) mean you may **never get a second chance** to clarify a confusing demo—**first 30 seconds** matter disproportionately.

- Advice to max out features with no **happy-path stability** plan.

RECOMMENDED STRUCTURE

Opening hook: You built something impressive, but judging felt random—usually the gap is **criteria literacy** and **demo time economics**, not talent.

Section 1: **How judging actually runs** — MLH expo vs Devpost online vs hybrid; **stack ranking vs star scores**; why **3 minutes** and **shortlists** change what wins.

Section 2: **Decode the rubric** — idea vs implementation vs impact (+ design/pitch when listed); **tracks/sponsor prizes**; **equal vs weighted** criteria (Devpost platform limitation).

Section 3: **What to optimize in 24–36 hours** — scoped MVP, demo script, submission asset checklist (video, repo, README proof).

Section 4: **Audience layer** — affinity hackathons and beginner tracks: **same rubric discipline**, different **community context** (still read the page).

The mistake: Optimizing for **your favorite technical rabbit hole** instead of the **published scoring rows** and **first-pass comprehension**.

Close: In the next 24 hours, **paste your event’s criteria into a checklist** and run a **3-minute timed demo** with a teammate who hasn’t seen the project; fix whatever they stumble on first.
