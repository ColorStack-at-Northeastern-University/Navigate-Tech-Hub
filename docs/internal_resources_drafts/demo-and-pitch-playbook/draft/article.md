# Demo and Pitch Playbook for Hackathon Finals

Finals are where good builds go to die because nobody rehearsed the first twenty seconds. The app works. The team is exhausted. Someone opens the laptop, starts explaining the database schema, clicks through three half-related features, and the judge still does not know who the project is for.

You do not need a TED talk. You need a short script, one demo path, assigned roles, and a backup plan for when the venue Wi-Fi chooses violence.

Hackathon demos are brutally short. MLH organizer docs often assume a two-minute submission video (demo, not slides), about three minutes per team at an expo table during judge debriefs, and roughly seven minutes per finalist slot on stage including changeover (often three to five minutes talking plus handoff time). Your hackathon might publish different numbers, so confirm limits in the Devpost, runbook, Discord, or whatever organizers actually sent. The principle stays the same: you do not have time to explain everything you built. You have time to make one thing obvious.

## Pick One Golden Path

Your demo should follow one user story from start to finish. Not every feature. Not every clever edge case. One clean path a judge can understand while standing in a loud room with twenty other teams behind you.

Use this test: if your project had to be explained in one sentence, what would the user do? "A student uploads a resume and gets three targeted fixes before applying" is a demo path. "We used AI to improve recruiting" is not.

That sentence becomes your golden path. Seed the account. Preload the tabs. Zoom in. Kill notifications. If your demo requires typing, paste from a note unless typing is the point of the product. Nobody gives bonus points because you successfully spelled a fake email address under pressure.

## Write Two Versions of the Same Pitch

You need an expo version and a stage version. They should tell the same story, just with different breathing room.

For a tight 2-3 minute expo pitch:

1. Problem in 15 seconds: who is stuck, and why does it matter?
2. Demo for 90 seconds: walk the golden path without detours.
3. Technical credibility in 20 seconds: name the stack or hardest part.
4. Impact in 20 seconds: who benefits, and what changes for them?
5. Close in 10 seconds: what you would improve next, or what you want feedback on.

For a 3-5 minute stage pitch, do not add five more features. Add context, slow the demo slightly, and explain one meaningful technical decision. The stage version should feel like the expo version with clearer lighting, not a different project.

Your recorded submission should match this story too. If the event asks for a 2-minute video, treat it as a demo-first artifact. Show the working product. Do not burn half the video on title slides and team photos unless the rules explicitly require it.

## Assign Jobs Before You Present

The worst demo role is "everyone sort of talks." It always becomes either awkward silence or four people interrupting each other.

Decide three jobs:

1. Driver: controls the laptop and does not improvise new routes.
2. Narrator: explains what the judge is seeing.
3. Timekeeper: watches the clock and gives a quiet signal when to wrap.

If you only have two people, combine driver and timekeeper. If you have four, the fourth person handles judge questions or jumps in for the technical line. Everyone should know the full pitch, though. Hackathons are chaotic. Someone might be getting food or debugging one last deployment issue when a judge walks up. Run the handoffs out loud. "I’ll take the technical part from here" sounds smooth only if you have said it before.

## Make a Failure Plan

Live demos fail in boring ways. Wi-Fi drops. OAuth gets suspicious. The projector crops your screen. Your local server stops. The judge asks a question mid-click and suddenly nobody remembers the point.

Have a fallback order:

1. Live app.
2. Local build or offline mode.
3. Screenshots in a folder.
4. A 30-60 second screen recording.

Ask organizers early about Wi-Fi, firewall restrictions, projector connection, microphones, and whether videos are allowed as backup. If you make finals, do an AV check if the event offers one. Bring the dongle. Someone always forgets it.

Write a tiny run-of-show: who opens, who drives, which account to use, which tab comes first, where the backup video lives, and the exact closing line. Put it next to the laptop. At 3 a.m., your brain is basically soup. Get the important details out of your head and onto the page.

## Practice With Someone Outside CS

Before finals, grab one person who is not on your team and does not know the project. Give them the pitch once. Then ask them to repeat three things:

1. What problem are we solving?
2. Who is it for?
3. What does the app actually do?

If they cannot answer, your opening is too vague or your demo starts too deep in the stack. Cut jargon. Move the user problem earlier. A judge with a software background still needs the human context before they care about the implementation.

You will also catch the line that sounded brilliant in the group chat and lands flat in person. Every team has one. Better to learn that before finals.

## Handle Interruptions Without Losing The Demo

Expo judges interrupt because they are trying to understand fast. That is not an attack on you, and one question does not have to wreck the whole flow.

Keep two phrases ready: "Great question. I’ll show that right after this 20-second flow." And: "We scoped that out for the 24-hour build. The next version would add it here."

Those lines buy you control without sounding defensive. Answer direct questions, but protect the golden path. If the judge never sees the main product work, they will remember the confusion more than the ambition.

## The 24-Hour Prep

Within 24 hours of presenting, do one timed dry run with the real laptop, real account, real tabs, and real handoffs. Then do one non-technical listener pass. After that, write down the three lines you refuse to improvise: the problem, the transition into the demo, and the closing impact line.

You can still polish code after that. Just stop pretending the pitch will magically assemble itself once the build is done. At finals, clarity is part of the product.
