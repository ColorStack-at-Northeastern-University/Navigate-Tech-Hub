# Hackathon Preparation System

Most first-year teams implode in the last ninety minutes while the editor still looks fine. Someone is still merging while another person discovers the demo has to be on YouTube or Vimeo, the repo was private, the thumbnail is the wrong aspect ratio, and nobody read the sponsor’s required API until hour twenty-two. You can ship reasonable code and still walk away with a half-finished submission form. Treat the portal (usually Devpost, or your event’s official submission site) like a second product you ship on purpose.

## Pick an on-ramp that matches your nerves

Hackathons usually cap teams at about four people. Judge conversations run short; some judges skim your repo, others dig into the demo. Placement is one outcome; skills and relationships still count when you do not place, especially if you are walking in convinced everyone else shipped three production apps in high school.

When you can, bias toward events that advertise beginner tracks, mentorship, and explicit “first hackathon welcome” language. Chapters sometimes run beginner-forward builds where community support is part of the pitch on the listing. ColorStack is a useful on-ramp for team-finding and moral support (ColorStack Family, sessions like Fam Fridays, summit-style programming such as Stacked Up Summit). Eligibility and event titles change; use [colorstack.org](https://www.colorstack.org/) and their news feed as the source of truth. Conference first-timer guides (for example AfroTech’s first-time attendee page) help with badges, schedules, and floor confidence. They do not replace a 24- to 36-hour build checklist. Keep conference playbooks separate unless you are explicitly bridging travel and networking with a hack weekend.

## Team contract before the repo goes wild

Pages from MLH and Devpost hammer the same lesson: everyone underestimates parallel workstreams. Before you argue about the idea for three hours, block thirty to sixty minutes to read rules, required technologies or theme, and judging criteria. That read is the gate that stops you from building the wrong thing.

Start team formation where organizers actually look: the Participants tab or official Discord. Post one line with what you can do, what you want to learn, and whether you have a seed idea or you want to join a team. Spread roles across the four-person cap: UI, backend shape, timeline honesty, submission narrative and demo story. Match tasks to stated strengths, not volume. If people have jobs or heavy course loads, say it and set milestones that assume real life.

First-hackathon writeups repeat the same survival kit: two or three idea tiers (ambitious, moderate, simple fallback), a wireframe or schema before novelty-chasing, and a stack you have touched before. A brand-new framework under a countdown is how you lose Saturday afternoon to dependency hell.

## Technical preflight this week, not at the venue door

Download your IDE, GitHub Desktop, and any heavy SDKs before you arrive. Venue Wi‑Fi often degrades once the room fills. Refresh Git basics enough that “clone, branch, merge, resolve” is boring. Decide who owns deploy, who owns the demo path, and who will be the person who actually clicks “submit.”

On Devpost, open “My Projects” early in the week and pre-fill teammates (emails or invite link), name, tagline, “Built with” tags including sponsor-required tools, and a gallery plan. Thumbnail shape and file limits (JPG, PNG, or GIF within posted caps) routinely eat the final hours. If the overview shows an eligibility-review style indicator, submit about a week early so organizers can flag wrong video hosts, licenses, or repo visibility while you can still revise. On other portals (Devfolio or an organizer-hosted form are common), assign the same owners early for description, demo link, repo visibility and license, roster, and sponsor-track boxes, then dry-run submit before the final hour.

## Build with integration checkpoints, not heroics

Time-box the weekend. Name check-in times when frontend and backend actually meet, when the demo script is read out loud, and when you cut scope without a guilt spiral. For in-person rooms, MLH packing guides return to the same short list: layers, a reusable water bottle, headphones or earplugs, deodorant. Never leave a laptop unattended; rotate who stays with gear at meals; leave bag space for swag. Add-ons that actually earn their weight: resume ready for sponsor uploads, one surge protector without daisy-chaining, portable charger, travel toothbrush, OTC pain meds only if that is safe and legal for you, parking confirmed with organizers if you drive.

## Submission is its own workstream

Assign owners early for four tracks that often collapse into one exhausted person if you are not careful: backend structure, frontend and UI, the written submission story (problem, approach, what worked), and the demo video. Proofread the form like it is an assignment. Make the repo public with a license that matches the rules. Host the demo on a host the rules allow. Read sponsor requirements like a grader.

If you want one page to tape above your desk: rules and judging criteria read by two people; portal draft started with teammates added; thumbnail and tags drafted; integration times on the calendar; owners for code, copy, and video; merge freeze; submit time that is not “minute fifty-nine of the hour.”

## Optional Northeastern-shaped anchors

Khoury’s events feed ([khoury.northeastern.edu/events](https://www.khoury.northeastern.edu/events/)) is a starting point; always open each hackathon’s own Devpost or organizer page for dates and requirements. PawHacks on the Oakland campus is described on Khoury’s site as a collaboration between Girls Who Code, Computer Science Coalition, and Entrepreneurs Club; participation can widen beyond NU while e-board expectations skew toward NU Oakland experience. In Boston, Center for Student Involvement runs Fall Fest and Winter Fest ([Fall Fest](https://csi.studentlife.northeastern.edu/fall-fest/), [Winter Fest](https://csi.studentlife.northeastern.edu/winter-fest/)) for org recruitment. One club page is not the whole university story.

## What to do in the next twenty-four hours

Register, join the community channel, start the submission draft on the official portal, and schedule a thirty-minute rules-and-criteria read with whoever might be on your team. If you are solo for now, do the read anyway and post your one-liner in Participants or Discord. The weekend goes smoother when the paperwork is bored on Thursday instead of screaming on Sunday.
