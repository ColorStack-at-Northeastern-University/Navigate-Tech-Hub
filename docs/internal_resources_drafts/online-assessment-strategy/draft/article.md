# Online Assessment Strategy for Timed Screens

You open the link, the timer starts, and your brain tries to solve problem one like it is the whole exam. Twenty minutes later you have a half-clean solution, two untouched prompts, and the sinking feeling that you just paid tuition to the platform instead of the employer.

You can know the patterns cold and still lose the screen when you cannot ship under the timer. What separates a pass from a mess is pacing and triage more than how many problems you have memorized.

## Before you click Start

Treat the invite and the rules screen like a contract. Read duration, number of sections, whether time is shared across questions, language options, proctoring, second-monitor rules, and what resources are allowed. Some flows explicitly allow syntax and documentation lookup online; others treat broad search or AI assistance as a hard violation. If the wording is ambiguous, assume the stricter interpretation until someone in recruiting clarifies. Do not improvise “environment hacks.” Watermarks, session recording, and device policies vary by company; reviewers compare candidates under the same integrity tooling.

If you are reading this for a **job** or vendor-hosted hiring screen, those rules win for that session. **Coursework** is different: follow each instructor’s syllabus and Northeastern’s academic integrity expectations ([catalog policy](https://catalog.northeastern.edu/handbook/policies-regulations/academic-integrity); [OSCCR mirror](https://osccr.sites.northeastern.edu/academic-integrity-policy/)). Khoury also frames [when AI is or is not allowed](https://www.khoury.northeastern.edu/khoury-college-faculty-on-when-and-when-not-to-use-ai-in-the-classroom/) at the course level. This guide is about hiring-style timed assessments, not substituting one policy for the other.

Pick the language you can type and debug fastest in, not the one you wish you were fluent in. Schedule the attempt when you are rested, fed, and unlikely to get pinged. Plug in the laptop, close notifications, and clear the desk. Sleep and focus are boring levers; they still move scores.

## First five minutes: read everything, then pick order

Skim every prompt, constraint, and scoring hint before you write production code. Label each item mentally as easy, medium, or unknown. If the platform shows point budgets or suggested ordering, believe them. On multi-item assessments, common shapes look like several tasks in one sitting with flexible time across questions while they stay unlocked; your job is to sequence work, not to marinate on the first ugly block.

Bank a clean win early. Finish the most tractable item first when the format allows partial credit across problems. That locks points and stops the timer anxiety spiral. If the UI lets you flag or revisit, use it as a literal bookmark so “come back later” is a plan, not a vague hope.

## Execution loop under time

When prose is dense, read sample input and output and any visible tests before you reread the story twice. Tests behave like a spec: they tell you what “correct” means under time pressure and surface edge-case intent faster than guessing from wording alone.

Start with something that runs, then upgrade. Brute force that passes visible tests beats a clever idea stuck in your head with zero submissions. If a clean approach is not emerging after a bounded slice of time (many students use roughly ten to fifteen minutes as a personal move-on trigger), switch tasks or downgrade scope. Zero output is the worst failure mode; a partial pass often is not.

At halfway on the clock, rebalance coldly. List what is still unsolved, drop perfectionism on the hardest block, and prioritize at least one runnable submission per required part if the format allows it. In the last ten minutes, bias toward “runs and submits” over cosmetic polish unless you are already green across the board.

## Platform realities without a fragile UI tour

Do one full dress rehearsal on the same vendor family you expect to see (for example HackerRank or CodeSignal style flows). You want the first ten minutes of the real attempt to be about the problem, not about finding the Run button, custom input, language switch limits, or how hints behave. Official help hubs change less wildly than random blog screenshots; for candidate rules and FAQs, start from the invite text, then [CodeSignal’s help center](https://help.codesignal.com/hc/en-us) or [HackerRank candidate support](https://candidatesupport.hackerrank.com/collections/9416206085-faq) if you need more detail than the email gave you.

If the assessment is AI-assisted, behave like you would on a real team: state your plan, ask targeted questions about edge cases and complexity, verify anything the model suggests, and own the approach. Some platforms log AI chat for reviewers, so “paste a full solution and hope” is a bad look even when the tool is enabled.

Retakes and how scores persist are product- and employer-specific. Do not assume you get a redo until the invite says you do.

## After the screen

Expect sparse or zero feedback. A single polite note to your recruiter about timeline or next steps is fine; repeated pings are not. If you need a mental reset before another company, separate what was format failure (UI panic, pacing) from what was skills failure (gaps in a specific topic) and train the bottleneck with one timed practice, not with another unfocused grind.

**24-hour action:** book one timed practice session in the same environment as your next invite, and run the five-minute triage for real once: skim all items, label difficulty, pick order, bank a win, set your move-on number before the clock starts.
